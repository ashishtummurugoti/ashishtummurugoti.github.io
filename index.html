MSc Dissertation: MBD Analysis of Conventional vs. Mode-Decoupled Suspension

Mar 2026 – Sep 2026

Project Overview

Suspension design plays a critical role in vehicle dynamics by influencing wheel motion, tyre loading, and handling balance. Conventional suspension systems couple heave, roll, pitch, and warp, limiting independent control over each motion mode. This project investigates a passive mode-decoupled suspension for a Formula Student car, using multibody dynamics to evaluate how separating heave and roll responses can improve roll control, lateral load transfer distribution, and overall vehicle behaviour.

Tools: MSC Adams Car, AVL VSM, C++, MATLAB

Methodology

The study is numerical. A full-vehicle MSC Adams Car model of the existing OBR26 car was built and validated, then used as the baseline for developing the OBR27 mode-decoupled suspension. Both cars were compared under identical conditions, from suspension level up to full-vehicle cornering, with a reduced-order yaw moment diagram as an independent check on handling.

Tyre Model Development

Developed a PAC2002 tyre model for the Hoosier R20 16.0×7.5-10 tyre in MSC Adams/Car using coefficients fitted from Calspan TTC experimental data through the MF5.2 tyre model. Defined the tyre’s force characteristics using measured slip angle and slip ratio sweeps across varying normal loads, inflation pressures, inclination angles, and road velocities to represent tyre behaviour under Formula Student operating conditions.

Pressure Sensitivity

Investigated the influence of inflation pressure on lateral force generation through slip angle sweeps at normal loads ranging from 210 to 1050 N. Compared four pressure conditions (55.16, 68.95, 82.74, and 96.53 kPa) to evaluate changes in lateral force across the operating range. Selected 68.95 kPa for subsequent tyre characterisation and full-vehicle simulations, balancing lateral force performance against the extreme low-pressure condition.

Inclination Angle

Evaluated the effects of tyre inclination angle on lateral force at the selected operating pressure of 68.95 kPa. Conducted slip angle sweeps at 0°, −2°, and −4° across normal loads of 210–1050 N to assess camber sensitivity. Increasing negative inclination generally increased lateral force, with the magnitude of the effect varying according to normal load and slip angle. These results established the tyre’s camber-dependent lateral response for subsequent vehicle dynamics simulations.

OBR26 Model Development and Validation

Developed a full-vehicle multibody model of the OBR26 Formula Student car in MSC Adams/Car, integrating front and rear suspension, steering, anti-roll bars, and a PAC2002 tyre model. Defined suspension geometry using vehicle hardpoint coordinates and incorporated measured spring stiffness and damper force–velocity characteristics to represent the physical vehicle.

Spring Stiffness

Spring stiffness was taken from the measured force–deflection data of each spring by linear regression of force against spring length. The front spring is 30 N/mm and the rear spring is 40 N/mm.

Damper Curve

The damper characteristic used in the model is digressive, with higher damping at low piston velocities and a reduced gradient at higher velocities. Rebound damping is greater than compression damping across the range.

Model Validation

The baseline was validated against the AVL model at the same ride height, static camber and toe, over a common wheel travel range of +29 mm to −29 mm, set by the damper stroke and motion ratio. Camber, toe, roll-centre height and wheel rate were compared.

OBR27 Model Development and Validation

Developed a partially decoupled suspension architecture in MSC Adams/Car using the validated OBR26 model as the baseline. Revised the front and rear suspension linkages with additional actuator hardpoints and joints to separate heave and roll damper motion while retaining the original wheel-guiding geometry.

Spring Rate Selection

Selected heave spring rates based on target sprung-mass natural frequencies and roll spring rates based on a target roll gradient of 0.88°/g, establishing separate stiffness characteristics for symmetric and differential wheel motion.

Heave and Roll Decoupling Validation

Validated the heave and roll decoupling behaviour of the front and rear suspension through parallel-wheel-travel and chassis-roll simulations. Evaluated wheel travel from −29 to +29 mm and chassis roll over ±1.5°, monitoring damper displacement to verify mode-specific actuation. Results confirmed that symmetric wheel motion primarily actuated the heave dampers, while differential wheel motion actuated the roll dampers, with minimal displacement in the opposing damping path.

  • The roll damper path can provide roll stiffness without a conventional anti-roll bar, which reduces component count.

2026 F1 Vehicle Development and Race Strategy Simulation

Jan 2026 – May 2026

Project Overview

Investigated the impact of the 2026 FIA Formula 1 regulations on vehicle dynamics and lap time performance using AVL VSM. Developed a baseline vehicle model and progressively optimised weight distribution, suspension stiffness, aerodynamics, powertrain parameters and handling balance. Used lap time simulation, G-G diagrams, yaw moment diagrams and vehicle response data to evaluate setup changes and identify trade-offs between outright performance and cornering behaviour.

Tools: AVL VSM, DRIVE Race, MATLAB

Baseline Vehicle Dynamics

Compared the 2025 and 2026 vehicle configurations using yaw moment diagrams to assess changes in cornering performance and handling characteristics. Established a 2026 baseline lap time of 90.742 s and optimised the static weight distribution to 44:56 (front:rear), improving lap time by 0.068 s.

Regulation Changes

The 2026 regulation changes that affect vehicle dynamics were applied to the 2025 car model. The narrower 2026 tyres were not modelled, because no 2026 tyre model was available.

At 200 km/h the 2026 car reaches a lower maximum lateral acceleration and a lower normalised yaw moment. The 30 kg mass saving is largely offset by the narrower track, which increases lateral load transfer. The dominant factor is the roughly 30% reduction in aerodynamic downforce. Because the narrower 2026 tyres are not modelled, the real performance loss is likely larger.

Aerodynamic and Powertrain Optimisation

Integrated aerodynamic and powertrain upgrades, then conducted parameter sweeps on suspension heave stiffness, static camber and differential locking. Increased front negative camber and differential locking to improve lateral grip and corner-exit traction, achieving a 0.949 s lap time reduction relative to the baseline. Identified increased high-speed understeer and rear roll-centre migration as key handling limitations.

Sweep Results

  • Differential locking: raising the static locking value from 45 to 395 N·m reduces inner-wheel spin and gained about 0.15 s, across several low-speed corner exits.
  • Front camber: more negative camber (−2.88° to −5°) added 0.1–0.2 g of lateral acceleration in each corner, worth about 0.23 s.
  • Heave stiffness: the new heave spring stiffnesses cut drag by roughly 0.1%, worth about 0.01 s.

Handling Limitations

The lowered ride height gave much more downforce, but at around 300 km/h the rear roll centre dropped below ground level. That creates excessive roll moments and negative jacking, which pulls the rear axle down under cornering. The understeer angle was also notably higher than in Iteration 1, especially at Turn 15, so a third iteration was needed.

Handling Balance and Variable Motion Ratio

Developed a speed-dependent rear suspension motion-ratio strategy to modify lateral load-transfer distribution across low- and high-speed corners. Combined this with revised rear heave stiffness and toe settings to reduce low-speed oversteer and high-speed understeer. Although the final iteration increased simulated lap time by 0.219 s, vehicle response data showed improved handling balance; the result highlighted the influence of driver-model behaviour on overall lap time.

Variable Motion Ratio

A stiffer rear heave spring kept the rear roll centre between 15 and 32 mm throughout the lap. Downforce compresses the suspension as speed rises, so rear wheel travel correlates with speed. That allowed a motion ratio to be mapped to speed, giving a higher front LLTD at low speed and a lower one at high speed.

Effect on Lap Time

With the variable LLTD the car shows less understeer in high-speed corners and less oversteer in low-speed corners. Unexpectedly, lap time rose by 0.219 s: the driver model did not apply throttle earlier, even though the steering wheel returned to a more neutral position on corner exit. Driver-model optimisation was outside the project scope, so it was not tuned. The improved handling would help reduce front tyre wear in a race. Small toe changes, toe-out at the front and toe-in at the rear, gained just under 0.1 s.

Limitations

  • The 2025 tyre model was used because no 2026 model was available. The narrower 2026 tyres would reduce grip and stability further, so the predicted performance loss is likely underestimated.
  • Inertias were scaled linearly from the 2025 car to meet the 770 kg minimum mass. This does not account for packaging changes in the 2026 architecture.
  • The driver model was not optimised at any stage, which leaves possible lap time gains unexplored.
  • The suspension kinematics needed to achieve the variable rear motion ratio were not designed. Adjustable-geometry rockers on the Aston Martin One-77 show the idea is practical, but the packaging was outside the project scope.

Vehicle Dynamics Modelling and Ride Optimisation for a Formula Student Car

Sep 2025 – Dec 2025

Project Overview

This project investigated vehicle handling and suspension performance using mathematical vehicle dynamics models, numerical simulation and parameter optimisation. The work progressed from a single-track bicycle model to a twin-track steering model, followed by quarter-car and half-car suspension models. Each model was used to study a different aspect of vehicle behaviour, from transient yaw response and steering geometry to ride characteristics, tyre load variation and pitch control.

The models are built on the TU Delft Formula Student car (DUT17), so the results can be checked against real Formula Student performance. Predictable handling matters because the drivers are students with limited racing experience, and consistent tyre loading is key to grip and competitive lap times.

Tools: MSC Adams Car, Python

Kinematic Bicycle Model and Handling

The bicycle model reduces the four-wheeled car to a single track, with the left and right wheels lumped into one at the centreline. It has three degrees of freedom: lateral velocity at the centre of gravity, yaw rate about the vertical axis, and a constant forward velocity. That keeps it quick to run for early design studies and parameter checks, while still showing how geometry, mass distribution and tyre properties shape handling.

The model uses mass and inertia data for the TU Delft car (DUT17) from Harsh (2018).

Tyre cornering stiffness uses a reference of 60–80 kN/rad for Hoosier tyres (Milliken and Milliken), scaled for load sensitivity. At the static load of about 650 N per tyre (265 kg, 50:50 weight distribution), a normalised stiffness curve gives a factor of about 0.71. Using the upper reference of 80 kN/rad, the baseline cornering stiffness is about 56,800 N/rad per tyre.

Vehicle Handling and Behaviour Study

Handling balance is described as understeer, oversteer or neutral steer. In understeer the front tyres saturate first, so the car runs wide, which is stable because slowing down restores front grip. In oversteer the rear tyres saturate first, which creates a yaw moment that can lead to a spin. In neutral steer both axles reach their limit together and the car follows the intended path.

The baseline car is neutral steer. To study the other two cases, the centre of gravity was moved ±76.5 mm along the car at a constant 265 kg. That changed the axle loads and tyre loads, and with them the load-dependent cornering stiffness. The understeer gradient K = (mg/L)(b/Cr − a/Cf) then gives the balance: K > 0 is understeer, K = 0 is neutral steer and K < 0 is oversteer.

Handling Model Results

The model was run with a step steer input, with a spline-interpolated tyre model to capture nonlinear force generation beyond the linear range. Understeer (US), neutral steer (NS) and oversteer (OS) were then compared.

The understeer car produces the highest front lateral force because of its higher front axle load, and the oversteer car the lowest. The understeer car has the lowest yaw rate, so it is the least responsive, while the oversteer car has the highest, so it is the most sensitive in yaw. Neutral steer sits between the two.

In the trajectories, the understeer car follows a larger-radius path and drifts outward, the oversteer car tracks a tighter radius, and the neutral steer car follows the nominal path closely.

Key Focus: Understeer and oversteer behaviour, transient response, nonlinear tyre forces and Python-based model development.

Extended Analysis: Twin-Track Model

The twin-track model extends the bicycle model with two wheels per axle. Modelling the left and right tyres independently captures effects the bicycle model ignores: lateral load transfer, asymmetric tyre forces and differences between inner and outer wheels in a corner.

Lateral Load Transfer

In a corner, lateral acceleration creates a moment about the roll axis that moves load from the inner wheels to the outer wheels. Ackermann and anti-Ackermann steering geometries were compared in a step-steer manoeuvre.

Ackermann gave a significantly higher peak load transfer, with the front outside wheel reaching about 2500 N against about 2200 N for anti-Ackermann. The larger steering angle at the inside wheel under Ackermann produces more lateral force and so a bigger moment about the roll axis.

Yaw Rate Response

Comparing the baseline and understeer set-ups under both geometries, anti-Ackermann gave a higher steady-state yaw rate in both cases. That means a more responsive yaw for a given steering input.

Validation Against Real-World Performance

The base model with anti-Ackermann geometry reaches a steady yaw rate of 77.1014 deg/s, which gives a skid pad lap of 360 / 77.1014, or about 4.67 s. TU Delft’s measured skid pad time was 4.606 s, so the model is 0.064 s slower, which is good agreement for a simplified model.

Suspension and Ride Optimisation: Quarter-Car Model

Quarter Car Model (1DOF)

The quarter car model isolates one corner of the vehicle to analyse basic ride characteristics. A step road input, like a sharp bump or kerb edge on a Formula Student track, excites the model so the transient suspension response can be evaluated.

When the bump is applied at 0.5 s, the body displacement rises sharply and peaks soon after, governed by the spring stiffness and damping. When the road input is removed at 1.0 s, the body undershoots and then oscillates with decaying amplitude back to equilibrium.

Quarter Car Model (2DOF)

Adding an unsprung mass gives a second degree of freedom. This captures the interaction between wheel, suspension and body, and separates ride comfort from road holding. Spring stiffness and damping can then be tuned to minimise body acceleration while keeping adequate tyre contact with the road.

A four-post rig input and step inputs were used. The four-post rig study looks for the configuration that minimises the RMS response, which is an objective measure of vibration severity and ride performance. A parametric study tuned the damping coefficient and damping ratio against two objectives:

  • Ride comfort: minimise the body accelerations transmitted to the driver.
  • Mechanical grip: minimise contact patch load variation, to keep tyre force consistent.

Four-Post Rig Results

2.3 → 2.0 dBPeak body acceleration
1.35 → 1.1 dBPeak contact patch load

The optimised damping reduces the peak body acceleration from 2.3 dB to 2.0 dB at the natural frequency of about 3 Hz. This limits resonant amplification and the acceleration passed to the driver during transient road disturbances.

It also reduces the peak contact patch load gain from 1.35 dB to 1.1 dB. The flatter response between 1 and 10 Hz means more uniform tyre loading across the road disturbances met in dynamic events, which improves driver confidence and lap time consistency.

Step Input Results

The step input produces a rapid rise, an overshoot and damped oscillations before the body returns to equilibrium, showing how spring stiffness and damping absorb and dissipate the energy of the bump.

Increasing the damping ratio or damping coefficient first reduces the spring force through better energy dissipation, but too much damping raises force transmission because the suspension motion becomes overly constrained. Increasing spring stiffness raises the spring force steadily, meaning higher load transmission despite less body motion.

A step input has no sustained frequency or amplitude content, so it is not suitable for RMS-based optimisation. It was used for qualitative and comparative analysis instead. The model was also tested with experimentally derived nonlinear damper data, comparing low and mid settings against a linear damper.

Key Focus: Ride response, damping optimisation, tyre load consistency, frequency-response analysis and nonlinear damper modelling.

Suspension and Ride Optimisation: Half-Car Model

The half-car model extends the quarter-car analysis to capture pitch dynamics and the coupled response of the front and rear suspension. It allows heave and pitch motion to be analysed together, giving a more realistic assessment of ride comfort and tyre–road interaction.

The model was used to tune the suspension for pitch control and for contact patch load variation at both axles. Key parameters were adjusted to reduce pitch motion while keeping tyre contact consistent, which balances ride comfort against handling performance.

Design of Experiments Results

Trial 57Optimal configuration for pitch control

A design of experiments varied front and rear spring stiffness and damping ratio. Trial 57 gave the minimum RMS pitch angle response. It is the best balance of front and rear suspension parameters for reducing pitch motion while keeping contact patch load variation stable.

Pitch Response

Compared with the base set-up, the optimised set-up shows a clear reduction in pitch amplitude and a faster decay of oscillations, which means better control of pitch motion under dynamic excitation.

Key Focus: Coupled heave and pitch dynamics, front–rear suspension interaction, design of experiments and suspension parameter optimisation.

Project Outcome

The project developed a structured understanding of vehicle dynamics across handling and suspension systems, using models of increasing complexity to investigate vehicle response. Combining time-domain simulations, frequency-response analysis, nonlinear tyre and damper characteristics, and parameter optimisation provided insight into how steering geometry and suspension settings influence vehicle behaviour.

EV Motorcycle Design: Gearbox, Suspension and Brake Systems

Apr 2025 – Aug 2025

Project Overview

The project covered the design of a compact multi-speed EV transmission for retrofitting into an ICE motorcycle chassis, an automated Arduino-controlled clutch and gear-shifting system, the rear suspension and the brake systems. Because everything had to work with an existing chassis, packaging and mounting limits shaped every design decision.

Tools: Arduino, stepper motor, linear actuator

EV Gearbox and Automated Shifting

I designed and developed a compact multi-speed EV transmission for retrofitting into a conventional ICE motorcycle chassis. The aim was to reduce the motor power and battery capacity the bike needs without compromising performance. A motor that can choose between several gear ratios does not have to deliver every riding condition through a single ratio, and the design had to fit the space and mounting points of the existing chassis.

The photo above shows the clutch and gear assembly of a conventional ICE motorcycle engine, the kind of constant-mesh drivetrain this work builds on. On a normal motorcycle the rider works the clutch lever by hand and the gear pedal by foot, so automating the shift means replacing both actions. I designed a clutch actuation system around a high-force linear actuator to disengage and engage the clutch. For the gear change itself I developed a shifting mechanism driven by a bidirectional stepper motor, so the gear selector can be moved in either direction for controlled upshifts and downshifts.

Arduino-based control logic coordinates the two actuators. It combines clutch actuation, shift motor control, gear-position sensing and limit-switch feedback, so each gear change follows a set sequence and the system can confirm that the shift has completed before carrying on. The prototype board below brings the hardware together: an Arduino Mega, a stepper motor with its gearhead and driver, a linear actuator with a separate motor driver, and the switch and sensor inputs.

To look at the control signals themselves, I recorded four of them on an oscilloscope over roughly a second. The capture below lets the timing of the signals be compared against one another, which matters when a clutch movement and a gear change have to happen in the right order.

Rear Suspension Design

I developed the rear suspension geometry and kinematics for the motorcycle, optimising the hardpoints, wheel travel, rake and spring rate. The main limits were the damper stroke and the packaging space available, so the geometry had to deliver the wheel travel needed while keeping the damper inside its stroke and clear of the rest of the chassis.

Static and dynamic load-transfer analysis under acceleration and braking then set the spring stiffness, damping rates and shock absorber requirements. Under hard acceleration weight moves onto the rear wheel, and under braking it moves off it, so the rear spring and damper have to cope with both situations.

The spring chart below compares two spring stages. K1 builds force steadily, reaching about 1,560 N at around 46 mm of total stroke, where it levels off. K2 only starts to act after that point and then rises, reaching about 1,200 N by roughly 77 mm of stroke.

The motion ratio chart shows how much the damper moves for a given wheel movement. It rises only slightly, from about 0.752 at −20 mm of wheel travel to about 0.769 at +80 mm. That is a change of roughly 2%, so the effective wheel rate stays close to constant through the travel.

Brake System Design

I designed and sized both disc and drum brake systems for the motorcycle, calculating the hydraulic pressure, piston dimensions, calliper forces and brake torque needed for each brake.

I then analysed the front and rear brake force distribution under different road friction conditions. The ideal brake curve below plots the rear brake force that matches each front brake force, for surfaces from low friction (μ = 0.1) to high friction (μ = 1.0). On a slippery surface only small brake forces can be used before a wheel locks, while on a high-friction surface the front brake can take well over 1,500 N, with the matching rear force falling as the front force rises.

A fixed brake force split is only safe if it stays below the ideal curve for the surface in use, so that the front wheel locks before the rear. Checking the front-to-rear distribution against these curves for different surfaces shows where a given split would let the rear wheel lock first.

What I Learned

Working across the transmission, suspension and brakes showed me how closely these systems depend on one another on a motorcycle. Packaging limits and damper stroke shaped the suspension geometry, load transfer under acceleration and braking drove the spring, damper and brake sizing, and automating a gear change meant the clutch, shift motor and sensors had to work in a strict sequence.

Aiming for a smaller motor and battery also moved effort into the transmission and its controls, which showed me that a design goal in one system often has to be met by changes in another. Building the control prototype and checking its signals on an oscilloscope added a hands-on side to what began as design work.

Study of Safe Booster Release

Jun 2023 – Aug 2023

Project Overview

Unmanned aerial vehicles are often launched with a booster that gives the aircraft its initial thrust. When the booster burns out it has to separate cleanly, and there is a real risk that it clashes with the airframe as it leaves. This project studied that release for a HEAT (High-Speed Expendable Target) UAV, a turbojet-powered aerial target used to practise weapon systems. It launches from a rail at 14°, with the booster mounted at 7.5° to the fuselage reference line, and the booster is released after 1.9 s of thrust, when the engine takes over.

I built a multibody dynamics model of the release in MSC Adams, modelling the joints, contact interactions and a time-dependent thrust profile, and tuned a custom spring force function to optimise release timing while keeping safe clearances. The work was done during an internship at the Aeronautical Development Establishment (DRDO), in the Structures UAV Division.

Tools: MSC Adams, HyperMesh, Microsoft Excel

The Aircraft

The target aircraft has a 3,000 mm fuselage and a 1,000 mm wingspan. It flies at up to 180 m/s and a maximum altitude of 5 km. The booster sits under the rear fuselage and is pushed clear by a spring once its thrust drops to zero.

Components of the Release System

The release involves six main components.

Booster Assembly

Provides the initial thrust for take-off.

Front Pin

The part the spring is compressed about. It transfers the booster thrust to the aircraft.

Bulkhead

A separate section of the aircraft that holds the stabilisers. It holds the front pin and connects the booster assembly to the fuselage.

Rear Pins

Act as the rear support for the booster bracket assembly, and slide to give guided motion during ejection.

Rear Pin Guide Way

Part of the fuselage that gives the booster a guide way, so it ejects smoothly by sliding on the rear pins.

Release Pin

Locks the booster in place while it is static. It falls away as soon as the thrust phase starts.

Simulation Setup

The model is purely mechanical, with no aerodynamic effects, and all components are rigid with no deformation allowed.

Joints and Contacts

  • Fixed joints (all six degrees of freedom constrained): fuselage to bulkhead, front pin to bulkhead, and each rear pin (port and starboard) to the guide way.
  • Contact forces to generate impact: booster assembly with front pin, bulkhead, release pin and both rear pins; release pin with bulkhead; booster cover with engine cover.

Contacts use the Adams impact function, where the force depends on penetration, stiffness, a force exponent, damping and a penetration depth over which damping ramps in.

Loads and Orientation

Booster thrust acts along the booster thrust line (BTL) through the booster centre of gravity, and engine thrust acts along the fuselage reference line (FRL). The booster is mounted at 7.5° to the FRL. Gravity is applied at 14° to represent the aircraft sitting on the launcher.

Spring Force Function

A standard Adams spring gave results that were not feasible for this release, so I wrote the spring force as a custom function of free length, compressed length and stiffness. The spring is represented by two markers, one in the hollow of the booster assembly (sm1) and one on the circumference of the front pin (sm2), and the distance between them is read at every time step.

StiffnessFree LengthInitial Compressed LengthPreload
23.25 N/mm31 mm25 mm≈ 140 N

The preload follows from F = K × dx = 23.25 × (31 − 25) = 139.5 N, rounded to 140 N. The force function is:

IF(DM(sm1,sm2)-31: 1, 0, 0) * (-23.25*(DM(sm1,sm2)-25) + 140)

  • While the markers are closer than the 31 mm free length, the IF term returns 1 and the linear spring force applies.
  • Once the booster burns out and the spring pushes the markers past 31 mm, the IF term returns 0 and the spring force drops to zero, so the spring stops acting as soon as the booster has been released.
  • For the 1.9 s of thrust, the booster thrust holds the spring compressed. When thrust falls to zero the spring force dominates and pushes the booster out.

The booster thrust profile was entered as a spline data element, interpolated to 2.2 s with the Akima spline fitting method.

Velocity and Acceleration

The aircraft reaches 71.94 m/s at 1.9 s. Acceleration holds a steady plateau of about 38 m/s² during the boost, then drops to about 9.25 m/s² once the booster thrust ends and the 40 kgf (392.4 N) engine thrust is carrying the aircraft.

Booster Thrust and Spring Force

Booster thrust stays near 2,700–2,900 N through the boost and falls to zero at 1.9 s. The spring force follows the same pattern at a much lower level, peaking at 334.4 N and falling to zero at release, which is what the IF condition in the spring function is designed to do.

Attitude

With the booster mounted off the fuselage axis, the aircraft rotates during the boost. At 1.9 s it has rolled 5.89°, pitched −14.85° and yawed 4.01°.

Clearance

The main safety check was that the booster does not strike the airframe as it separates. At 1.9 s the clearance between the booster cover and the engine bay is 8.3 mm, and between the booster cover and the fuselage pin holder it is 43.445 mm. Both stay positive through release and then open up rapidly as the booster leaves.

What I Learned

I ran over a thousand simulations in MSC Adams on this and related tasks. The hard parts were the spring force function, because the standard spring did not give feasible results, and getting the contact properties and moments of inertia of the bodies right. I solved these through self-study, research and a lot of iteration. It taught me how sensitive a multibody simulation is to every input, and that the real-world behaviour is only reproduced when each parameter is accounted for.

I also learned to write my own force functions, move data from Excel into Adams, and use HyperMesh for meshing, topology and shape optimisation, and linear static analysis. I gave the organisation my simulation data and a detailed report on every Adams function I used.
